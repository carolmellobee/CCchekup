I built Ledger because I wanted a page that lets you understand an analytics dashboard by using one, rather than by reading about one.

Most explanations of dashboards are written for people who already have the data. You get a screenshot, a list of chart types, and no way to find out what any of it would say about your own situation. That gap is what this is aimed at. You bring a file you already have — a credit card statement, locked, exactly as your bank sent it — and the page turns it into the thing being explained. The dashboard and the tutorial are the same object.

The subject comes from years of studying and building data analytics visualisation. The technical foundation comes from my work in data engineering and AI. I'm interested in the question the RAG and LLM era has made unavoidable: when a system produces an answer, how does the person on the other side judge whether to believe it? Retrieval-augmented systems answered that with citations. A dashboard has no equivalent convention, so I tried to build one here.

What that looks like in practice

Every number on this page is meant to be traceable back to the line in the PDF that produced it. The Statements tab has a parser view that highlights precisely which lines were read as transactions, so a wrong total is a thing you can see rather than a thing you have to trust. Each file is reconciled against the invoice total the statement itself declares — if the parser and your bank disagree, the page says so instead of quietly showing a plausible number.

The forecast tab follows the same rule. Four models compete on your own history, each scored by walk-forward backtesting, and the error of each one is shown next to its prediction. When a three-month median beats a trend model, that tells you something real about your spending. The most reliable line on that chart isn't a forecast at all — it's your remaining instalments, which are already contracted and need no model.

How it actually works

Worth being plain about this, because the words above could suggest otherwise: Ledger does not call a language model and does not perform retrieval. It is deterministic — pdf.js decrypts and extracts text, rule-based patterns identify transaction lines, keyword rules assign categories, and the forecasts are ordinary least squares, Holt exponential smoothing, month-of-year seasonality, and a robust median, combined by inverse-error weighting. The LLM connection is in the design question, not the implementation.

Privacy

Statements are among the most revealing documents a person owns. Nothing here is uploaded: files are read in the browser, PDF passwords never leave the page, there is no backend, no account, no analytics and no logging. Nothing persists between sessions either, which is a real cost — export to JSON before you close the tab if you want to keep your work.
